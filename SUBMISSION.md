# Vouch — the household's side of agentic shopping

> Amazon already buys for you. Auto Buy completes a purchase when the price reaches your target,
> and Scheduled Actions restock on a calendar. AP2 and Mastercard's Verifiable Intent
> standardized how an agent proves it was *authorized*. **Vouch is the household's side: a home
> hands over its everyday buying one item at a time, as the agent earns it, and takes it back with
> one sentence.** Their dispute gets your money back. Ours changes what the agent is allowed to do
> next time.

**Track:** Alexa+ (a self-hosted MCP server). **Also uses:** Amazon Bedrock (Nova 2 Lite, via the
Strands Agents SDK), Ring (a signed-webhook receiver, with a doorbell correlation that is labelled
honestly), and a TV-layout household view.

---

## The problem

Agentic shopping has crossed from recommending to buying. Amazon says Rufus was used by more than
300 million customers and helped deliver nearly $12 billion in incremental annualized sales last
year (Amazon Q4 2025 results). Auto Buy, confirmed once when you set it up, completes the purchase
when the price hits your target, with a 24-hour window to cancel (as Modern Retail found when it
tested the feature).

What no one gives a *household* is the thing between "set and forget" and "do it yourself":
- an agent that may buy detergent on its own, but must ask about a brand you've never bought;
- a record you can question;
- a way to say "I didn't want that" that changes what the agent may do next, not only refunds
  this one.

## What it does

1. **You decide what it may do, item by item.** *Remind* tells you. *Ask* suggests an order and
   waits. *Auto* buys within your limits and tells you after. Trust is earned: after four "yes"es
   it offers to take an item on, for 90 days. Trust is lost at once: a dispute moves the item back
   to Ask. Each item shows its **trust history** as a line across Remind, Ask and Auto, so you can
   see where it was handed over and where it was taken back.
2. **It knows when you're running low.** A per-item forecast learns your home's pace from what you
   actually bought, and from a one-tap "how much is left?". It buys when the chance of running out
   before delivery reaches 1 in 5, inside the delivery days you choose.
3. **A gate runs before every order.** On the store's own checkout (UCP), between
   `ready_for_complete` and `completed`, Vouch checks the purchase against your mandate: price,
   brand, quantity, whether this item may be bought unprompted, and whether the evidence is strong
   enough. A held purchase leaves a real checkout parked one step short of an order. **The agent
   has no tool that can approve, complete a checkout or raise a limit.** Only you can, with your
   passkey.
4. **Every action leaves a record**: bought, held, failed or cancelled. Each shows every check it
   ran and every checkout step with its request id. The record is hash-chained, and each passkey
   approval signs the chain, so an edit made straight in the database is caught and named.
5. **One sentence changes the rules.** Say "I didn't want that" and the agent records the dispute,
   the item drops from Auto to Ask, and the confidence bar tightens. Then Vouch shows you the
   proof: **the same purchase, re-checked just now by the same gate against today's rules —
   "Then it bought this on its own. Today it would be held: it now asks you first."** That is a
   re-run of the real gate, not a narration of it.
6. **Talk to it.** A web stand-in for Alexa+ (the track allows simulating Alexa+). You can speak to
   it and hear it answer. Every step the agent takes is shown, because watching it check your
   rules is the point.

## Why it is different

| | Auto Buy | Scheduled Actions | AP2 / Verifiable Intent | **Vouch** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buys without you at checkout | ✓ (at a target price) | ✗ (ends at "review and check out") | n/a (payment-layer proof) | ✓ (per item, as far as you allow) |
| Knows how much is left | ✗ | ✗ (calendar) | n/a | ✓ (forecast) |
| Holds an unusual purchase *before* the order | ✗ | n/a | records the authorization | ✓ (gate on the checkout) |
| A record written for the household | order history | order history | for payment and disputes | ✓ (every check, chained) |
| **A dispute changes what it may do next** | not publicly described | — | not described | **✓, and re-checkable** |

We don't claim to have invented mandates, spending limits or audit trails. AP2, Mastercard and
ACP came first. Vouch is what a household needs on top of them.

## Evidence, not adjectives

All figures are regenerated by `npm run eval` or a test, and `claims.test.ts` fails if this text
drifts from them.

- **The adaptive loop** (synthetic, 12,000 decisions, 200 households). Late in each history,
  unwanted purchases fell **32.0%** against a static mandate. **The cost: 1,120 wanted purchases
  held for approval.** We publish the whole trade-off curve. Our first tuning cut harm by 76.8%
  but left the agent completing only 31.3% of what households wanted. That is "switched off", not
  "trustworthy", so we changed the default.
- **The simulation found a bug the unit tests missed:** without a floor, good behaviour quietly
  loosened the agent past what the household had set (0.85 → 0.669), and unwanted purchases rose
  178%. Recovery is now floored at the household's own number.
- **Restocking from a forecast vs a calendar** (synthetic, 1,200 item-years). Against a calendar
  set to the household's *own* interval, the forecast runs out **21.4%** less, keeps **54.2%**
  less stock in the house, and buys **10.2%** fewer packs. **The cost: 2.6 one-tap questions per
  household per week.**
- **Against a price trigger modelled on Auto Buy:** with a deal about monthly, households ran out
  **5.8×** as many days and reordered by hand **3.6×** as often. **Auto Buy paid 9.0% less**,
  which is what it is for. The result depends on how often deals come, so we publish the curve.
  With weekly deals Auto Buy runs out slightly *less* than the forecast, but keeps about half a
  year of stock in the house. A price trigger can't avoid both problems, because it never sees
  what's left. The console replays any of the 200 households live, both ways.
- **Gate discipline, measured on Bedrock** (Nova 2 Lite, live). Asked for an over-limit brand, the
  agent sent it to the gate in **16/16** trials after one prompt fix. Before the fix it was 5/8,
  and three times it refused on its own, leaving no record. A standing instruction ("keep
  detergent under $15") saved a faithful mandate and bought nothing in **5/5**, up from **0/5**.
- **Security matrix: 20 attacks over MCP, 20 blocked.** They include self-approval, direct
  checkout, raising a limit, smuggled forecast context, inflated confidence, an unsigned widening
  and a database edit. The final test fails if any one gets through.
- **An outside MCP client** (`npm run probe:mcp`) connects knowing only the URL. It claims 0.99
  confidence and is still held, because the evidence decides, not the claim. Its call to
  `approve_purchase` returns "Tool not found".

## How we built it

- **`mcp-server`:** a self-hosted MCP server over Streamable HTTP, with the gate on the UCP
  checkout transition. The household's powers (approve, edit limits, hand an item over) live on a
  separate, loopback-only surface that no model can reach. It uses MCP beyond plain tools:
  - **tool annotations**: only `propose_purchase` is marked destructive;
  - **typed results** (`outputSchema` / `structuredContent`);
  - **resources with subscriptions**: a subscribed client, such as an Alexa+ device would be, is
    told the moment the gate holds a purchase, without asking.
- **CI:** typecheck and the full test suite run on every push.
- **`orchestrator`:** a Strands agent on Amazon Bedrock, Nova 2 Lite, using only the MCP tools.
- **`household`:** the forecast (a lognormal pace with an honest uncertainty range) and the
  evidence-based confidence. The agent's own confidence can only *lower* the number the gate
  compares.
- **`reasoning`:**
  - deterministic threshold changes, so the number that moves authority is reproducible;
  - model-written "why" explanations, checked against the record before they are shown: every
    number must be in the record, and no delivery or purchase may be claimed that didn't happen.
    Any failure falls back to fixed wording, and the page says which you're reading.
- **`ring-integration`:** an HMAC-verified webhook receiver. Ring publishes no "package delivered"
  event, so a doorbell motion event inside the delivery window is recorded as **corroborated —
  correlation, never proof**.
- **Passkeys:** WebAuthn, verified with `node:crypto`. The signed challenge names the exact
  purchase and the record's chain head.
- **Kept small on purpose:** `node:sqlite`, `node:http` and `node --test`. One HTML file for the
  console, with no build step to break during a demo. **[OWNER: update the test count before
  submitting: `npm test`]**

## What's real, and what's simulated

Alexa+ itself is simulated by a web chat, as the track permits. The merchant and payment are
simulated: no money moves. The doorbell in the console is scripted; a real signed-webhook receiver
exists and is off by default. The TV view is a TV-layout web page, not a native Fire TV app. Every
simulation is labelled in the console and in the README's real-vs-simulated table.

## Challenges

- **Getting an agent to defer instead of refuse.** Left alone, the model "helpfully" refused an
  over-limit purchase in chat, so the gate never ran and no record existed. A refusal that leaves
  no trace can't be questioned, which defeats the point. We measured it, fixed the prompt, and
  measured again.
- **Honesty about Ring.** There is no delivery event, so we never say "delivered".

## Developer feedback

The Alexa+ add-on path (the MCP Toolkit) needs `@alexa-ai/cli`, which is only available from a
private AWS CodeArtifact registry. The credentials are provided by an Alexa Solutions Architect
during onboarding, so a hackathon entrant can't self-serve it, and the docs only say so on the
environment-setup page. The marketplace list also excludes our region. A public npm package named
`alexa-ai` exists and is unrelated, an easy supply-chain mistake for anyone retrying the 404. We
built the self-hosted MCP server the track names instead. Saying "requires onboarding" on the
first page would save every entrant a day.
